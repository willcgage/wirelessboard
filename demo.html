Screens & docks

The displays this installation runs on, and what sits underneath them. Measured once, for every board.

A board keeps its columns wide enough to line up with the microphones in the dock below it — a column narrower than a dock position stops pointing at anything. Measure the diagonal, the size it was sold as; the resolution is not asked for, because every screen reports its own.

Docks

What is on the table below a screen. Everything down there is a dock, whether or not it charges: a charging dock, a networked one that can also be asked what is in it, or a non-charging dock where microphones are simply left. All of them are rows of positions and the board treats them the same.

Measure the width of one position, left to right — what a column points at. ⛔ Not one bay: an SBC200 has two bays, front and back, and they line up with a single slot — the microphone in front, that performer’s IEM behind it. The venue’s Shure SBC200 is 3.9in. ⚠️ Since the one-row arrangement became a guarantee, this width decides how many columns a board shows.

Slots

Who is on each channel. The person is the only thing typed here — the receiver reports everything else, and the device id is an override for when it is wrong.

Planning Center writes both of these when it is connected — a plan says “Bob Roberts — Vocal 1”. Typing them by hand is not a fallback: it is what a board without Planning Center uses permanently, and what any board uses for somebody a plan does not name. ⚠️ A sync overwrites a row it names, and leaves one it does not name exactly as you typed it. ⚠️ The × on a row is the deliberate way to take somebody off a slot — an empty box clears a name, so a stray delete should not be the way it happens.

How positions match slots
How somebody reaches a slot
Planning Center positions carry the mic name and number — a person under position “Vocal 1” fills the slots labelled for mic 1 and IEM 1.
Single-team plans only. Keys on the trailing number alone, so with several teams scheduled “Vocal 1”, “Guitar 1” and “Host 1” all claim the same slot.
A sync normally only writes names, so whoever was scheduled last week stays on their slot until a later plan replaces them. With this on, a sync takes the person off any slot the new plan has nobody for — including one you assigned by hand. The slot keeps its ID, so it still matches next week.
Without this, the bar appears and waits for somebody to press Sync. With it on, the board syncs the change itself — including, if “Clear slots this plan does not fill” is also on, taking people off slots while nobody is watching. A slot two people both claim is never guessed at: it is left exactly as it is and flagged for a person. Needs the checkbox above.
⭐ A rule set can turn this on or off for its own kind of plan — Youth automatic, the main service by hand.

Rule sets

Different kinds of plan, with their own rules. A rule set changes only what it names — everything else is inherited.

⛔ A template cannot be bound to: Planning Center copies a template into a plan rather than linking it, so nothing the board can read says which template a plan came from. That is why a rule set can also be picked by hand for one sync.

Rule sets for different kinds of plan
Sunday morning, Wednesday youth and a special service rarely want the same rules. A rule set changes only what differs from the settings above — everything it does not mention it inherits, so a later change here reaches all of them.
Bind one to a service type or to a folder (which covers every service type inside it) and it applies on its own. Leave it unbound and you pick it when syncing — which is how a Christmas Eve service is handled, since Planning Center copies a template into a plan rather than linking it, so nothing the board can read says which template a plan came from.

Shows where the service has got to — the current item, its timer, and what is next. ⚠️ A server setting, not a per-screen one: it spends one Planning Center account’s allowance on behalf of every board, so it is off until somebody turns it on. Each screen then chooses where to put it, or to hide it, in its own view settings.
Live is often not running, and the strip says so plainly rather than showing a stale position as though it were current.
Planning Center’s own three modes, with its own names and behaviour. Full item length counts the item’s scheduled length. End item on time counts to when this item was meant to finish, so a late service shows as late. End service on time counts to the end of the service. Past zero the timer turns over and counts up, exactly as Live does.

Taking the photographs

                

                  A channel card is always taller than it is wide — often much taller. A portrait photograph fills it exactly; a wide one cannot, and the board fills the space around it with a blurred copy of the picture rather than cutting the face off. That always works, but a portrait source needs no filling at all, so it is worth getting right once.
                

                
    
                  
  • Shoot portrait, about 3:4 or 4:5 — 1200×1600 is ample. A phone held upright is already the right shape.
    • 
                  
  • Head and shoulders. Aim for the face to fill roughly a third of the frame’s height. A full-length stage shot leaves a face a few pixels across on the wall.
    • 
                  
  • Leave a little headroom and keep the person near the centre. A head against the top edge is the first thing lost when a card is short.
    • 
                  
  • One recognisable face, looking toward the camera. A profile, a back of a head, or a face behind a microphone or a cymbal reads as nobody from five feet away.
    • 
                  
  • Even, front-ish light. Stage lighting from behind turns a face into a silhouette, and the board cannot brighten what was never captured.
    • 
                

                

                  Photographs taken on a phone keep their rotation correctly, so an upright shot stays upright. Wide stage photographs still work and nothing will be cropped away — they simply sit smaller, inside a blurred band. If the board is showing twelve channels in a single row, every picture is a narrow strip whatever its shape; Grid is the arrangement photographs are at their best in.
